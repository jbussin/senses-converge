What is being tested

Identifiability theory says that contrastive learning with local positive pairs recovers the latent structure behind its inputs, up to a simple transformation. If that holds for real networks, two learners that perceive the same world should converge on its shared structure even when they share no training data. That is one proposed explanation for the Platonic Representation Hypothesis, whose evidence comes mostly from models trained on overlapping human data. A constructed world separates the two explanations.

The learning rule decides whether convergence happens

At full overlap, the three rules without drift end in the same place. The truth columns show why: the eye moves toward the true structure under every rule, but the ear moves only with drift. Cross-model agreement needs both.

Alignment with the true shared properties at full overlap (S = 8, width 32, 8,000 steps, mean of three seeds). Hollow circle: untrained network, shared by all rules. Filled circle: trained. The right column is the eye↔ear alignment of the same pair.

How much drift?

Too little drift approaches the no-drift case; too much makes the two glimpses nearly unrelated objects. The prediction, written into the script before it ran, was an inverted U. It held in every seed, and the ear depended on drift more than the eye in relative terms (3.3× against 1.75×).

S = 8, width 32, 8,000 steps, mean ± SD over three seeds. σ = 0 is contrast_fresh; the shaded column is σ = 0.05, the value used in the main experiments. Tripling training at σ = 0.05 raises calibrated eye↔ear alignment only from 0.070 to 0.086.

Detectable is not usable

If the two representations share neighborhoods, it might be possible to recover which image goes with which sound without any pairs. We gave each learner's embeddings, pairing discarded, to Gromov–Wasserstein matching. It stayed at chance for every drift and seed. Running the same matcher on the true latents against noisy copies of themselves shows why: it needs far more agreement than the learners have.

Fine agreement fades as the evaluation grows; coarse agreement holds

Koepke et al. (2026) report that language–vision alignment falls as evaluation sets grow while coarse category agreement survives. Partially shared perception produces the same pattern. With more objects, each object's nearest neighbors are decided by finer differences, and private properties, which the other sense cannot see, decide more of them. Measured at a fixed fraction of the data instead of a fixed 10 neighbors, agreement stays flat.

Trained learners: calibrated eye↔ear alignment (contrast_drift, σ = 0.05, three seeds, 10-permutation null). A sanity check with language and vision models

On the 1,024 image–caption pairs from the original study, calibrated alignment between Pythia and DINOv2 still rises with language-model size, so the reported trend is not an artifact of the layer search. This regime is known to overstate convergence and shares human data on both sides, so it is a pipeline check, not evidence about scale.

Mutual 10-NN alignment, maximized over layer pairs and calibrated against 20 permutations that repeat the full layer search. Every cell beat all 20 permutations. Cross: randomly initialized Pythia-410M, whose best layer is always the token embedding.

Predictions and outcomes

The core predictions follow from identifiability theory and were fixed before the multi-seed runs. The three follow-ups were designed after the main results, with predictions written into each script before it ran.

Limitations: a toy world with 8 independent uniform latents per sense; networks of a few million parameters; three seeds; one neighborhood metric and one matching method; most results at 1,024 objects with one image and one sound each. The results show that a shared world can produce convergence without shared data, not how much of the convergence between web-trained models it explains.
